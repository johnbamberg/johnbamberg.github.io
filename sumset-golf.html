THE MATH BEHIND THE GAME

Six is the floor.

The Cauchy–Davenport theorem guarantees at least 4 + 3 − 1 = 6 distinct sums.

A score of zero is perfect, but some sets A make it impossible. Can you find the best score for this hole?

Arithmetic modulo a prime.A little addition. A lot of possibility.

WELCOME TO THE COURSE

Make the sums overlap.

  1. Share a set. Each hole gives everyone the same four-element set A.
  2. Choose three cards. Your three distinct residues form B. Cards may also belong to A.
  3. Add every pair. A + B contains all a + b, reduced modulo the prime. Repeated sums count only once.
  4. Keep it small. Your score is the number of distinct sums minus 6. Lowest total wins.

For two players on one device, look away during the other player’s turn. Choices are hidden during the handover and revealed after both players lock in.

CAUCHY–DAVENPORT

A bound, not a promise.

For nonempty subsets A and B of the field with p elements,

|A + B| ≥ min(p, |A| + |B| − 1).

Here |A| = 4 and |B| = 3, so there must be at least six distinct sums. That makes every score nonnegative.

The bound need not be attainable for a given A. In this game, a score of zero is possible exactly when A is an arithmetic progression modulo p; a progression B with the same step then works. The converse follows from Vosper’s theorem.

For example, modulo 17, A = {1, 4, 7, 10} and B = {0, 3, 6} give A + B = {1, 4, 7, 10, 13, 16}. Six sums, score zero.

After a hole, “Show best play” displays a B with the smallest possible sumset, found by checking every choice up to translation.
